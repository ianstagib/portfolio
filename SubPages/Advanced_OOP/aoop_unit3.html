Unit 3: Design Patterns I – Creational Patterns

In brief: I built the brief’s car manufacturing system in Python with the Factory Method pattern, with 26 tests. The readings use ‘factory method’ for three different things, and writing the Gang of Four version showed that it only earns its extra classes when the creator has work of its own. It moves the choice of class to one place rather than removing it.

This unit introduces design patterns, proven solutions to recurring design problems, and studies the five creational patterns: Singleton, Factory Method, Builder, Prototype and Abstract Factory. It looks at how they are used in real systems and asks for an implementation of the Factory Method pattern. My car manufacturing system, its tests and a walkthrough notebook are stored below as the unit’s artefact, followed by the 300-word report the individual activity asks for and my reading notes.

Reflection

Structured using the What? So what? Now what? model (Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper, 2001).

What?

Unit 3 introduced design patterns, starting with the five creational patterns. I read Parts 1, 2 and 4 of Sarcar (2022), concentrating on chapters 2 to 7 and 28 to 30, chapters 3, 9, 10 and 12 of Reddy (2024), chapter 5 of Oliveira (2023) and chapters 1 and 2 of Soshin (2024). For the exercise I built the brief’s car manufacturing system in Python with the Factory Method pattern: an abstract Car, three cars, an abstract CarFactory with create_car(), three factories and a demonstration, with 26 tests (Artefact 1).

So what?

The first surprise was that the readings do not agree on what a factory method is. Oliveira (2023) uses a static createInstance(), Soshin (2024, ch. 2) a function whose when expression picks a class from a letter, and Reddy (2024, ch. 3) a CreateRenderer() that takes a type string. Sarcar (2022) separates these. He treats the string-driven version as a Simple Factory (ch. 2) and keeps the Gang of Four name for creators whose subclasses decide what to instantiate, conceding that even his own NumberFormat.getInstance() example is ‘not a perfect example’ because no subclassing is involved (ch. 3, Q&A 3.7). Most of what I would have called a factory method before this unit was a simple factory.

Writing the brief’s version made the difference concrete. With only create_car(), SedanFactory().create_car() does nothing that Sedan() does not, and in Python a class is already a callable that returns an instance. The pattern earned its place only when CarFactory had an operation of its own: produce() calls the factory method, refuses anything that is not a Car and stamps a serial number, the role createAndDisplayAnimal() plays in Sarcar’s second demonstration (2022, ch. 3). The cost is the one Sarcar names in Q&A 3.3: a parallel hierarchy with a factory class for every product.

The brief asks for a main program that creates cars without naming their classes, but something still has to choose SedanFactory. In my design that choice sits in a read-only table used by factory_for(), which is a simple factory with Reddy’s registry (2024, ch. 3). The client functions are free of concrete classes, and a test adds an electric car without editing cars.py, yet the decision has moved to where the program is assembled rather than disappearing. That is the same thing I found with dependency inversion in Unit 2.

The lessons on Singleton were mostly warnings. Reddy (2024, ch. 3) and Sarcar (2022, ch. 7) both cite Gamma wanting to drop it, and Reddy offers dependency injection as the alternative. My Unit 2 action plan was to replace a class-wide counter with an injected generator, and the serial numbers were the chance to do it: SerialNumbers is passed into each factory, so every test gets its own sequence. Sharing it across factories makes it shared mutable state, so, following Reddy’s Thread-Safe Interface pattern (2024, ch. 9), only the public next() takes the lock. Nine factories in parallel threads issued 2,700 serials with no repeats.

Two limits stood out. Oliveira (2023) and Soshin (2024, ch. 2) make constructors private so that the factory is the only way in, and Sarcar (2022, Q&A 2.2) argues that creation must be centralised for consistent life-cycle management. Python cannot enforce this: Sedan() skips produce(), its check and its serial, and a test records it. From a security angle, a factory is a natural control point for validation only if callers cannot go around it. Second, Reddy (2024, ch. 10) warns that adding a pure virtual method to a published base class breaks every client subclass. My test shows Python accepts such a subclass and fails only when it is instantiated. The factories hide concrete classes from the client, but they do not protect anyone from changes to Car.

Was the pattern worth it here? Sarcar (2022, ch. 28) notes that many patterns are now built into languages and that small applications pay for them in code size, and Soshin (2024, ch. 2) shows Kotlin’s object, copy() and named arguments doing most of the work of Singleton, Prototype and Builder. Python is similar. For three cars, a dictionary of classes would have met the brief. Factory Method justifies itself when the creator holds logic that every product passes through, as in Sarcar’s iterator() example from the Java collections, not merely to avoid writing a class name.

Now what?

Before reaching for Factory Method I will ask whether the creator has work of its own; if not, a simple factory or passing the class is enough. I will keep running one set of contract tests over every registered factory, as I did with payment methods in Unit 2. Next I will extend the exercise to an Abstract Factory, for example UK and US market factories that each produce a car and its matching handbook, to test Sarcar’s point that a change to an abstract factory spreads to every concrete one (2022, Q&A 4.2). I will also look for factory methods in the Python standard library to see which form it prefers.

Artefact 1: A Car Manufacturer Built with the Factory Method Pattern

Individual Report: Applying the Factory Method Pattern

The 300-word solution the activity asks for, excluding code listings and references.

Problem

A car manufacturer builds Sedans, SUVs and Hatchbacks and expects to add models. If the main program constructs each car directly, every new model means editing that program, and the program depends on every concrete class. The Factory Method pattern addresses this by letting subclasses of a creator decide which class to instantiate (Sarcar, 2022, ch. 3).

Design

Car is an abstract class with the abstract method drive(). Sedan, SUV and Hatchback inherit from it and each override drive(). CarFactory is an abstract creator with the abstract factory method create_car(), which SedanFactory, SUVFactory and HatchbackFactory override to return their own car (Figure 1, above). CarFactory also defines produce(), which calls create_car(), rejects anything that is not a Car and records a serial number. Because produce() is written against the abstraction, every factory inherits the same checks, and client code depends only on Car and CarFactory, which applies the dependency inversion principle.

class CarFactory(ABC):
    @abstractmethod
    def create_car(self) -> Car: ...

    def produce(self) -> BuildRecord:
        car = self.create_car()
        ...

class SUVFactory(CarFactory):
    def create_car(self) -> Car:
        return SUV()

Demonstration

The client function road_test(factory: CarFactory) builds and drives a car without naming any concrete class. main() turns names such as ‘suv’ into factories through one read-only lookup table, so the choice of class is made in a single place. Adding a model means one new entry in that table rather than a change to the client.

CAR-000001 Sedan: a smooth, quiet ride on the motorway
CAR-000002 SUV: high driving position and four-wheel drive on a gravel track
CAR-000003 Hatchback: nipping through town and into a tight parking space

Evaluation

A new model needs a new car class and factory class but no change to existing code, which keeps the design open for extension. Twenty-six tests confirm each requirement and show that the client code names no concrete class. The cost is an extra class for every product (Sarcar, 2022, ch. 3). Python also has no private constructors of the kind Oliveira (2023) uses in C++ to make the factory the only route to an object, so a caller can still construct Sedan() directly and the factory relies on convention.

References. Oliveira (2023); Sarcar (2022). Full entries are in the reference list below.

Reading Notes

Sarcar (2022), Parts 1, 2 and 4 (required). Each Gang of Four pattern in Java, with a definition, a real-world and a programming example, a demonstration and a Q&A session. Part 1 covers the SOLID principles (chapter 1) and the Simple Factory (chapter 2), which is not one of the Gang of Four patterns and breaks the open/closed principle, since adding a product means editing its if/else block. Chapters 3 to 7 cover the creational patterns. The Factory Method chapter uses a car manufacturer as its real-life example and adds a method that calls the factory method and then runs code common to every product, which is the structure I used for produce(). The Q&A sessions are the most useful part: the cost of one factory subclass per product, the parallel creator and product hierarchies, why a static getInstance() is not strictly a factory method, and how a change to an abstract factory spreads to every concrete factory. Chapter 7 compares lazy, eager, holder-class and enum versions of a Singleton in Java and ends with Gamma’s view that its use is ‘almost always a design smell’. Chapters 8 to 25 cover the structural and behavioural patterns, which later units return to. Part 4 (chapters 28 to 30) covers criticisms of patterns, including that languages now absorb many of them and that small programs pay for them in size, then anti-patterns and an FAQ comparing similar patterns.

Reddy (2024), chapters 3, 9, 10 and 12 (required). A C++ book about designing libraries for other programmers, which makes it a useful outside view of patterns as part of a public interface. Chapter 3 covers the pimpl idiom, Singleton and its alternatives (dependency injection, Monostate and a single session object), factory methods as a way round the limits of constructors, a registry-based extensible factory, and the Proxy, Adapter, Façade and Observer patterns. Chapter 9 covers concurrency: data races, stateless APIs, safe initialisation of shared data and the Thread-Safe Interface pattern, which takes locks only in public methods. Chapter 10 covers versioning and compatibility, including the rule not to add pure virtual methods to a published base class. Chapter 12 covers testing, including test-driven development and stub and mock objects, and presents dependency injection as what makes them possible.

Oliveira (2023), section 5 (additional). Chapter 5 applies Factory Method, Singleton, Observer and Visitor to options trading in C++. Its factory method is a static createInstance() on a class whose constructors are private, used to hide where market data comes from. Its Singleton example is a clearing house, the one institution an application trades through. The chapter is short and presents each pattern favourably, so I read it mainly as an example of patterns in a specific industry rather than as an evaluation of them.

Soshin (2024), chapters 1 and 2 (additional). Chapter 1 introduces Kotlin’s syntax and closes with a short account of what design patterns are, rejecting the idea that they are only missing language features or only for object-oriented languages. Chapter 2 covers the five creational patterns in Kotlin and keeps showing the language doing the work: the object keyword gives a Singleton, companion objects give static factory methods, default and named arguments make most builders unnecessary, and a data class’s copy() is a Prototype. His Factory Method example is a function that chooses a chess piece class from a letter, which Sarcar would call a Simple Factory.
